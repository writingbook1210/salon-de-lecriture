Story of Dreams (letter form)

幾島乃起

この作品は英語で書かれています。

To you,Daiki Tashiro

On Wednesday, in the quiet of the library, I came across a book about balloons. Have you ever wondered how they work? As hot air rises above cold air, the basket, held by the fabric, slowly lifts into the sky. To be honest, before I looked into it, it seemed almost magical. Thinking about it, though, it seems like a rather simple principle, and I felt a bit of disappointment. I find balloons resemble the act of knowing. My desire to know has no limits, just its sole purpose is to ascend. The fact that the amount one can know in life is finite also resembles a balloon. Humans are shaped by experience, someone once told me. Because humans can know, because they have daily experiences, their minds store the world through the five senses and enrich their ideas. Then, not knowing is the same as being dead. Not knowing something new is stagnation. A stagnant river ceases to be a river, merely water that goes nowhere, halting its flow to await siltation. The world is full of things I do not yet know, far exceeding all that I could ever learn in a lifetime. Sometimes, when I think of this, I feel a sense of fear.

I dreamed one day that, as usual, I was inside one of their bodies. It is a planet orbiting a large red dwarf star, where light is extremely weak and visible light scarce, and gravity and tidal forces are strong. The planet's surface consisted of a semi-liquid layer of minerals and organic matter, an environment where information was more readily transmitted by changes in gravity than by light. The aliens’ tentacles touch the semi-liquid surface like delicate brushes tracing the space. Though the light is dim and almost nothing is visible to my eyes, I can grasp the surrounding environment as if I could see it. The tentacle tips read the slightest tremors and distortions in space as three-dimensional information. As I understand it, they “listen” to changes in gravity, much like bats perceive terrain through ultrasound, sensing the surrounding conditions from the ripples in space.

You once asked me before about the language of those aliens. They maintain a strange continuity across distant lands, much like the Indo-European languages on Earth. Yet it did not branch through separation or isolation, as that continent did. Quite the opposite: they possess a nature that is continually on the move. When separated groups meet again in different places, they exchange their “way of reading” gravity each time. The language blends before branching, and journeys on in that blended condition. Thus, their vocabulary does not split apart, but instead seeps into one another like a thin membrane.

Their conversation is accompanied by a peculiar stillness; merely a slight tremor of their tentacles leaves tiny ripples in the space. It is neither sound nor light, but transmits as a minute shift in gravity. Though invisible to the eye, for them it serves as both speech and writing, and at the same time as a record. These ripples exist in the fourth dimension, unlike the two-dimensional, non-reversible forms found in our languages. It is not something I can grasp in its entirety. Yet as I observe them, I can sense, even if only a little, that in their world, reading space with their tentacles is thinking, ‘talking’ is learning, and recording is prayer.

I've been wondering about this for a long time, about the desire for knowing. Where it originates, where it resides, and where it flows. I mentioned that knowing resembles a rising balloon, and if so, I feel I should explore the source of that “desire to know” within me. Surely, it is an element distinct from knowing itself. “Knowing” is an action and its outcome, while “wanting to know” is a matter of the heart, isn't it? Perhaps my very core can be understood by seeking that out. Since then, I have actively been reading the poetry of others – a gathering of knowledge to expand who I am. Conversely, because relying solely on my own language feels insufficient, I’ve also reached out to poetry from other countries. I particularly enjoyed an anthology of Chinese Tang poetry, where I discovered the profoundly striking ‘Thoughts on Parting’ by Yuan Zhen (古文岛, n.d.).

曾经沧海难为水，除却巫山不是云。

"He who knows the great sea cannot be satisfied with mere water. He who has seen the clouds of Mount Wu cannot regard any other clouds as clouds.". I did not realise at first that this poem was one of love. Yuan Zhen had lost his wife, and knowing this changed my entire perception of it. Both the “sea” and the “clouds” are metaphors for moments with his wife, singing that for someone who has already passed away, nothing holds value except the time spent with her. Simply knowing the context gives vivid colours to just a few dozen characters. I thought, after all, that knowing is truly wonderful. What should one call this sort of thing? It’s absolute and without error. Surely, it is a beautiful light that can touch anyone, if they make the effort to seek it.

I cannot help but think of the vessels they called “The Place”. They have five round legs and give an impression of simplifying the aliens' appearance. Its sides are covered with countless holes, which I could not at first tell whether were breathing pores, recording windows, or both. In my view, crafting the vessel and inscribing knowledge are two sides of the same act for them. The vessel's material is a semi-liquid clay that hardens to stone over an extremely long period. The sensory organs at the tips of their tentacles interact with the gravitational field to shape it. The most familiar analogy for us would be a book. The vessel is crafted as a device to impart knowledge to those who touch it, much like inscribing words upon pages. Its height, size, and the placement and dimensions of its holes are not determined by blueprints or plans, but are determined naturally by the sensations of the tentacles.

When I try to carve the knowledge into the Vessel with them in my dreams, a sense of awe and excitement naturally comes up. The sensitive sensation of the gravitational field, the subtle movements of the tentacles... they teach me nothing. It is not through being taught, but through the very act of touching, feeling, and shaping, that I learnt. It is as if I am experiencing how to learn itself, much like what Bateson calls ‘deuteron learning.’ The world itself becomes the place of learning, the university, as Tim Ingold describes. Here, it is not only those belonging to specialised disciplines or faculties who learn. The geologist learns not only from the professor but from the rock itself; the botanist learns from the plant; the ornithologist learns from the bird. And anthropologists learn from the people they stay with (Ingold, 2013). In the world of the vessel, for me, the tentacles, the materials, the space itself are the teachers and the subjects of learning. Making, touching, feeling – these acts themselves are the ritual that inscribes knowledge, as if I am experiencing learning itself.

Kojiro Yuichiro, in Forms in Japan, categorises the actions involved in traditional Japanese craft according to how they engage with materials and processes. He distinguishes acts that create connections, such as tying, binding, and weaving; acts of gathering, like collecting, stacking, and felting; acts of arranging, including combining, scattering, and discarding; and acts involving flow, such as dripping, flowing, and smearing (Adamson & Buszek, 2007). These categories focus attention not on the finished object but on the ways in which making itself evolves – the gestures, movements and interactions in which knowledge is inscribed. Considering the process of creating the vessel, this philosophy resonates remarkably well. The Vessel is not merely a shaped object; it embodies the very act itself. It engraves knowledge as the tentacles converse with space, shaping form while sensing gravity and mechanics through their sensory organs.

Yet interestingly, the more knowledge the vessel holds, the more it lights up from within. It was as if a candleholder with tiny holes held a flame inside, flickering and breathing. The light spilling from each pore felt as though it were telling the recorded moments and thoughts. Much like how each character in a book seems to speak with its own voice to the reader. It seemed almost the only light visible on that planet. For them, knowledge is not mere accumulation, nor cold data, but light. Light that lingers, slowly leaves warmth in those it touches. It somehow reminds me of that quiet, deeply rooted sense of fulfilment I feel when reading poetry.

The soft light remains within me even after waking from the dream. Have you ever read Zhuangzi? The vivid sensation left on my fingertips, the feel of tentacles touching the Vessel like turning pages makes me lose the sense of whether I am an Earthling dreaming of being an alien, or an alien dreaming of being an Earthling. Tell me about your dreams someday, too. That star, that sky, even this Earth - perhaps they are all just paint, and nothing is quite as we believe.

Reference

Ingold, T. (2013) Making: Anthropology, archaeology, art and architecture. London: Routledge.

Paterson, E.C. & Surette, S. (eds.) (2015) Sloppy Craft: Postdisciplinarity and the Crafts. 1st edn. London: Bloomsbury Academic.

古文岛. (no date) “曾经沧海难为水，除却巫山不是云。《离思五首·其四》”, 古文岛. Available at: https://www.guwendao.net/mingju/juv_e87e6b55ba7e.aspx [Accessed: 10 November 2025].
