Anger

たしろだいき

I sometimes feel anger. It can be triggered by everyday events, society, other people, or even myself. When you hear the word ‘anger’, you might imagine someone shouting and ranting, but I have rarely been able to express myself in that way. Looking at the emotion of anger closely, it is difficult to find the exact words, but it is like a quiet snake that stirs quietly deep within me. It is like rain drops falling on a still lake deep in the forest. I have two cats, and one of them, a black-and-white cat named Gina, has a black spot about five centimetres in diameter on her upper palate, one that is only just visible when she opens her mouth wide. Anger is often likened to bubbling magma, isn’t it? Mine feels a little different. It’s a sensation of a cold wind passing through. A chill, like the breeze tracing the rim of a gaping hole. Like a cat’s tail, it follows me from behind, something I can neither catch nor make disappear. That is what anger means to me.

Story of Dreams (letter form)

たしろだいき

On Wednesday, in the quiet of the library, I came across a book about balloons. Have you ever wondered how they work? As hot air rises above cold air, the basket, held by the fabric, slowly lifts into the sky. To be honest, before I looked into it, it seemed almost magical. Thinking about it, though, it seems like a rather simple principle, and I felt a bit of disappointment. I find balloons resemble the act of knowing. My desire to know has no limits, just its sole purpose is to ascend. The fact that the amount one can know in life is finite also resembles a balloon. Humans are shaped by experience, someone once told me. Because humans can know, because they have daily experiences, their minds store the world through the five senses and enrich their ideas. Then, not knowing is the same as being dead. Not knowing something new is stagnation. A stagnant river ceases to be a river, merely water that goes nowhere, halting its flow to await siltation. The world is full of things I do not yet know, far exceeding all that I could ever learn in a lifetime. Sometimes, when I think of this, I feel a sense of fear.

The ‘Fluid Nature’ of Photography in the Digital and Social Media Age

How has Photography Evolved from a ‘Technology for Preservation’ to ‘Content for Consumption’?

たしろだいき

Today, with the widespread use of smartphones and technologies, humans are capturing a great number of photographs, however, how often in the course of a lifetime do we actually look back at those photos: the picture of lunch you took a few weeks ago, the sunset you saw on your way home, or the selfie with a friend from a few years back? The author's smartphone contains no fewer than 16,074 photos and 802 videos, amounting to 126.9GB of data , yet the vast majority of these remain sunk into an SSD soldered onto the circuit board, never to be looked at again. Images gathered in smartphone folders, Instagram Stories that expire within 24 hours, perhaps the very nature of photography has shifted from being ‘something that preserves memories for the future’ to ‘symbols consumed in the present moment’. Instead of deeply experiencing the scene in front of us, we press the shutter button (or simply the screen) to produce a symbol indicating that we were present at that place at that moment.
