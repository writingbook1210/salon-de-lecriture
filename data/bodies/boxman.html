From a wide window facing south stands by my pillow, the morning sunlight invariably wakes me at seven o’clock. Within five seconds, I sit up and glance around the bedroom I find myself in. Was it Aachen in Germany? I wonder about the place where he was born, the designer of a chair in which straight lines and curves blend seamlessly, with soft leather stretched over a metal frame. In front of it, a desk peeks out, composed solely of chrome legs and a thick tabletop, resembling twin brothers or the A-side and B-side of a cassette tape.

I then leave the bedroom and head to the kitchen, exactly twenty-one steps. I pick up one of the stainless steel measuring cups lined up on the wooden shelf, pour 150 millilitres of tap water into a glass, and drink slowly. As for the beautiful plants by the window - I begin by using a pipette to drop water, one drop at a time, onto the base of the Sansevieria. For the olive tree, I gently give it about 15ml, just enough to make the surface slightly damp (it’s dormant). For the Tradescantia, I pour only 10ml down the edge of the soil, taking care not to wet the leaves. I give the Rhipsalis hanging on the wall a light spray from a misting bottle, ensuring only the stems and tips of the leaves are slightly damp. I cast a glance at the cats.

Measure out 155ml of tap water for the Bialetti Moka Pot (three-cup size). From the jars of coffee beans lined up on the shelf, select the Burundi Bourbon variety. Weigh out exactly 16g using the scales, grind it in the manual stainless steel grinder, then level it out and pack it into the basket. Before the gas hob finishes brewing the espresso, I mustn’t forget to prepare breakfast. I take a slice from a stack of white bread, each slice cut to a thickness of one centimetre, lined up like a row of dutiful soldiers. I carefully make a hole with a radius of 3.5 cm in the centre and place the slice in a frying pan with 5 ml of olive oil. I crack an egg carefully, from an angle of 45 degrees, let it settle slowly into the hole. I sprinkle a suitable amount of salt and pepper over it, cover the pan, and wait patiently until it is thoroughly cooked. Just like a bear in deep hibernation, eagerly awaiting the snow to melt. Parmenides’ words - ‘Nothingness does not exist’ - cross my mind. Was it Aristotle who regarded the hole as part of the ‘Eidos’? Indeed, this hole serves as a comfortable chamber for the egg to rest in.

Just as the hands of every clock in the room simultaneously point to eight o’clock, the Box Man knocks on the front door. Three slow knocks, one second apart. When I open the door, the Box Man slowly holds out the cardboard box he is clutching to his chest. The box looks terribly worn, yet at the same time it looks as good as new. It reminds me of an intricate lenticular print where, the moment you shift your perspective ever so slightly, a completely different image emerges. His movements are slow and awkward, as if he were an intricately crafted automaton using up the last turn of its mainspring. The Box Man’s face - if indeed one could call it that - lacked any of the features one would expect to find there; instead, there was a distorted void. That blackness was darker than the night, darker than a collapsar, and deeper than any well you could imagine. ‘The things you try to forget are the very things you can never forget.’ said the Box Man. I nodded and took the box he held out to me, each side measuring about fifty centimetres.

(Inspired by Haruki Murakami and Kōbō Abe)
