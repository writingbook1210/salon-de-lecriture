I sometimes feel anger. It can be triggered by everyday events, society, other people, or even myself. When you hear the word ‘anger’, you might imagine someone shouting and ranting, but I have rarely been able to express myself in that way. Looking at the emotion of anger closely, it is difficult to find the exact words, but it is like a quiet snake that stirs quietly deep within me. It is like rain drops falling on a still lake deep in the forest. I have two cats, and one of them, a black-and-white cat named Gina, has a black spot about five centimetres in diameter on her upper palate, one that is only just visible when she opens her mouth wide. Anger is often likened to bubbling magma, isn’t it? Mine feels a little different. It’s a sensation of a cold wind passing through. A chill, like the breeze tracing the rim of a gaping hole. Like a cat’s tail, it follows me from behind, something I can neither catch nor make disappear. That is what anger means to me.

When I was little, I came across the word ‘修羅 (Shura)’ in a book, and it stayed in my mind for quite a long time.

During the summer holidays, I would hole up in the small town library and spend my childhood there from morning until evening. I loved buying a cream bun and curry bread from the local bakery, and my mother used to give me some pocket money for that. The library had a wide variety of books: novels, poetry collections, and all sorts of specialised books I was too young to understand. Among them, my favourites were the works of Kenji Miyazawa. The more accessible, children’s-oriented pieces from his collection were published as slim volumes, making them easy for a child like me to pick up. I believe they included ‘The Restaurant of Many Orders’, ‘The Nighthawk Star’, and ‘Night on the Galactic Railroad’. As the number of books I’d finished grew and my options narrowed, there was one genre I never quite got round to trying: poetry collections. Though I now chew over poetry like a caterpillar, honestly it was at first something I did not fully understand. It was in one of the poetry collections I eventually picked up that I came across the word ‘Shura’. The reason I remembered it was because there were six little Kṣitigarbha statues in the cemetery on my way to school, and I used to gaze at the stone engraved with the word ‘Shura’ for no particular reason.

He suggests that one’s own existence is not something fixed, but merely a ‘phenomenon’ that is constantly changing, flickering on and off within one’s relationships with others and the world. From his poetry, I can sense the Buddhist concepts of ‘諸行無常 (Syogyo Mujo - All things are subject to change)’ and ‘縁起 (Engi - Dependent origination)’ also strongly reflected here.

Kenji Miyazawa’s 'Haru to Shura (Spring and Shura)', published in 1924, was the only collection of poetry he produced during his lifetime; however, he did not regard it as ‘poetry’, as indicated by the title 'Mental Sketch Modified', it was a record that faithfully captured the scenes reflected within his inner self.

Spring and Shura, the title of this collection of poems presents two contrasting elements that symbolise the world of the works.

Spring: Symbolises the radiance of life, a sense of vitality, the beauty of nature, and Kenji’s longing for the utopia (Ihatov) and the world of the Lotus Sutra that he sought.

Shura: Derived from the ‘Shura Realm’ in the Buddhist doctrine of the Six Realms, a world filled with strife and anger. This symbolises Kenji’s own inner conflicts and worldly desires, his inability to escape arrogance, self-loathing and anger whilst seeking an ideal, as well as the harsh and rugged natural landscape of the Tohoku region where his hometown is.

I’ve been seeing Shura within myself. I harbor feelings of anger and resentment towards that side of myself, the part that suffers from desire and confusion, wounding others whilst wounding itself, yet sees those emotions walk away the moment a delicious dinner is set in front of me. I feel the same way towards others, too. When I look at blank faces that appear to be thinking of nothing and indeed are thinking of nothing, I feel as though I am being pushed by a flood of anger, or perhaps by the wind. Ultimately, I suppose, this anger is linked to loneliness. Others cannot know the world I see, nor can I know theirs. I hold agnostic or Kantian views and am not a Buddhist, yet it seems I have not truly grasped the concept of 諸法無我 (Anatta - All phenomena are devoid of self).

Upon rereading what I have written, I realised it did not quite fit the assignment’s brief, so I will try writing about anger in a more down-to-earth way. I work part-time at a small Japanese restaurant in Tamka, and I get really annoyed when customers come in just five minutes before closing time. This is not some introspective or metaphysical anger; I’m simply irritated. It is a practical, straightforward kind of anger. Once the shop closes, I want to tidy up and clean as quickly as possible so I can get home early. The customer places an order. If it's one or two rice balls, I would make them within only three minutes, but I hate making takoyaki as it takes over ten minutes. I tell them, ‘I’m afraid we’re already closed. Niestety.’ Perhaps fatigue can be a factor. Above all, I’m just a part-timer; no matter how much we earn, it does not really benefit me, which is why I couldn’t care less.

This concludes my description of anger.
