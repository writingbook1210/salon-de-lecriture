1. Introduction: The Digital Transformation of Photography

Today, with the widespread use of smartphones and technologies, humans are capturing a great number of photographs, however, how often in the course of a lifetime do we actually look back at those photos: the picture of lunch you took a few weeks ago, the sunset you saw on your way home, or the selfie with a friend from a few years back? The author's smartphone contains no fewer than 16,074 photos and 802 videos, amounting to 126.9GB of data , yet the vast majority of these remain sunk into an SSD soldered onto the circuit board, never to be looked at again. Images gathered in smartphone folders, Instagram Stories that expire within 24 hours, perhaps the very nature of photography has shifted from being ‘something that preserves memories for the future’ to ‘symbols consumed in the present moment’. Instead of deeply experiencing the scene in front of us, we press the shutter button (or simply the screen) to produce a symbol indicating that we were present at that place at that moment.

A study by psychologist Linda A. Henkel (2014, 401) found that when participants took a photograph of an exhibition after viewing it, they remembered fewer exhibits and had poorer recall of details and locations than when they simply observed them without taking a photograph. She points out that the act of taking a photograph enables people to erase the subject from their memory, thereby making them rely on the camera and photos as an external device to ‘remember it for them’. This finding highlights the fundamental difference between human memory and the recording provided by a camera.

As Henkel suggests, the moment we outsource our memories to an external device like a camera, we may be granting our brains a Menzaihu: ‘You no longer need to remember this yourself.’ Ironically, the more vividly a 48-megapixel Sony's digital sensor records a scene, the more our own vivid memories are stripped away. In this paper, I explore the background to the transformation of photography from a ‘preservation for the future’ to a ‘consumer good of the present’, and examine what technology is bringing to human cognition and society.

2. Historical Foundations: From Film to Data

Before diving into the discussion of digital photography, let us first explore the historical foundations of the question: what did taking and viewing photographs (or visual culture in general) actually mean for humans in the first place? This is because, since the invention of photography, the human act of ‘seeing’ has become inextricably linked to technology. Let us reflect on the days of film (though, having been born in 2003, the author has no personal memory of them). There were bulky cameras, strict limits on the number of frames and the time-consuming process of developing in a darkroom or laboratory. This series of procedures may well have created a certain ‘temporal distance and solemnity’ between the photographers and the subject. A photograph was used to be a physical object; until it is developed, it lies hidden as an invisible image on a plastic film approximately 130 microns thick. In his 1990 book "Techniques of the Observer", Jonathan Crary prophetically pointed out the decisive transformation brought about by the rapid development of digital technologies, including computer graphics, as follows.

He argues that the rapidly advancing and diverse range of computer graphics technologies over the space of just over a decade forms part of a comprehensive reconfiguration of the relationship between the observing subject and the mode of representation, effectively undermining much of the cultural significance of terms such as ‘observer’ and ‘representation’. And importantly, most of the historically significant roles of the human eye are being supplanted by methods in which visual forms no longer hold any reference whatsoever to the observer’s position within the real, optically perceived world. (Crary 1990, 1–2)

What Crary discusses connects to the very essence of the images we capture and consume on our smartphones. Photography once served as proof of a physical and spatial relationship: ‘a person standing there with a camera, optically capturing the world’. In the digital world, however, images are severed from both the actual world and the observer’s physical body, and this is because what appears on the screen is nothing more than an array of data, a sequence of zeros and ones that can be edited, duplicated or deleted at any time.

3. The Cultural Shift: Photography as Communication

In today’s fully digitised world, this ‘quantification’ is being pushed to new extremes; the cost of taking photographs has effectively been reduced to almost zero, enabling us to generate and reproduce an infinite number of images. This technological transformation is fundamentally shifting the very domain that photography captures. In the era of analogue photography, the subjects of photographs were limited to records of non-everyday moments: weddings, holidays and anniversaries, however, with the dawn of the digital revolution, photography has been democratised to document ‘trivial fragments of everyday life’. What we capture on our smartphones today are neither historic moments nor formal family portraits, but rather a plate of lunch that will disappear (both physically and digitally) within hours, the gradations of a passing sky, or perhaps a note made as a reminder.

Regarding this approach to photography, José van Dijck (2007) argues that digital technology has brought about a radical transformation in the cultural function of the act of photography itself. Where analogue photographs were typically kept in ‘someone’s album or shoebox’, prioritising the preservation of past moments for future reflection, photographs in the context of digital cameras and social media can be viewed as functioning as tools for 'identity and communication’ to shape ongoing relationships with oneself and others in the present. He also argues that, in the modern era where image editing using software such as Photoshop has become commonplace, it is necessary to reconsider the primacy of photography as a tool for memory (van Dijck 2007, 98–99) In other words, photography has transformed from a ‘medium that physically preserves the past’ into a ‘fluid language’ for sharing the present moment with others and presenting the self in real time.

So, what does photography, now transformed into this ‘tool of communication’, bring to our lives? Susan Murray (2008) identifies a similar phenomenon in her analysis of the initial impulse behind online image-sharing platforms such as Flickr. According to her, the practice of sharing digital images has transformed the snapshot from an artefact of long-term memory into an immediate self-expression of the present moment (Murray 2008, 155). This shift towards ‘instant sharing’ is bringing about an internal transformation in the very way we frame our own lives. As Murray points out, contemporary users are using cameras to transform the trivial routines of daily life into aesthetic objects. This is not merely a matter of recording one’s life; it is nothing less than the act of reorganising and presenting one’s daily existence as a ‘curated moments’. (Murray 2008) We are not pressing the shutter button to create an album of the future; rather, as Murray and Dijck have described, we are pointing the camera in order to enter the ongoing flow of the aesthetics of the everyday, which circulates intensely through the network, and to instantly consume and perform the ‘sign’ of our presence in that place.

4. The Performative Nature of Modern Digital Consumption

When digital photography becomes a language of real-time communication, or a performative consumer product in the present tense, a decisive reversal occurs in the very structure of meaning of human experience. Contemporary people do not simply experience something and press the shutter button as a way of capturing the emotional resonance of that moment.Instead, within the bloodstream of the social media network, they are working backwards to select experiences whose value is guaranteed in advance, in order to gain the reaction of approval from others. This behaviour of modern people, in which experience is reduced to mere symbols, shares a significant correspondence with the dynamics surrounding tourists and cameras, as discussed by Theopisti Stylianou-Lambert in her 2012 paper. Lambert points out the passivity of those holding cameras, noting that they are not simply gazing naively at the reality before them, but are merely tracing ‘images as symbols’ that have already been constructed by society and the media. People can only photograph what they ‘must’ photograph; just as there are places and monuments one ‘must’ visit, there are photographs one ‘must’ be captured, and these are posited as cultural concepts (Stylianou-Lambert 2012, 1819）

The act of everyone photographing landmarks from the exact same angle on holiday, or the repetitive, déjà vu-inducing compositions of food photographs constantly shared on social media, are not records of personal, internal memories; rather, they are a semiotic appeal to others that declares, ‘I have correctly consumed this symbol circulating in society’, and amount to nothing more than the replication of a code. These are predetermined ‘stages’; when we overlay this with Bourdieu-ish photographic practice bound by social roles, and the cycle of this reproduction, Lambert asserts that ‘there is left almost no room for conviction or creativity’ for the contemporary photographer. （Stylianou-Lambert 2012, 1820–21）

The clear conclusion of this essay would be, in the digital environment, photographs have completely stopped being static objects rooted in an individual’s inner memories and experiences, instead, they function as a currency (consumer good) for the mutual circulation of immediate, quantified evaluations such as ‘likes’ and ‘reactions’ on social media.

5. Conclusion: The Passive Observer in the Digital Flow

In this essay, the author has tried to trace the process by which photography in the digital environment has moved away from its former privileged status as a ‘tool for preserving the future’ and has transformed into a fluid ‘currency’ through which the present moment is consumed in mutual exchange with others. Beginning with Henkel’s (2014, 401) concept of the outsourcing of memory, and moving on to Crary’s (1990, 1–2) discussion of the quantification of vision and its detachment from the body since the nineteenth century, the symbolic and aesthetic performances of everyday life discussed by van Dijck (2007) and Murray (2008), and Stylianou-Lambert’s reflections on passivity through the ‘hermeneutic cycle of reproduction’ (Stylianou-Lambert 2012, 1820–1821), what has emerged is the figure of the contemporary ‘passive observer’, having lost all spontaneity and floating on a sea of codified images. However, the liquefaction of the medium of photography and the accompanying hollowing out of human experience are not limited to the game (or Rat Park) of endlessly accumulating signs on the perpetual timelines of Instagram and TikTok. In recent years, at the forefront of image sharing, pictures have begun to reject archiving even more explicitly, functioning literally as one-off communications, which is evidenced by the rise of ephemeral media platforms like Snapchat, and the one-time-view photos sent via Instagram Direct Messages. These environments leave users with no room to look back at the past, thus, in this ephemeral space where images reject even the accumulation of validation (like!), and are instantly erased, photography seems released from what Murray describes as the immediate performance of the present (Murray 2008) and from the pressure of constructing a permanent identity. However, the author ponders whether the reality of the situation might signify a growing dependence on visual language and the increasingly instrumental nature of communication, such that our generation, Generation Z ‘can no longer even exchange emotions of the here and now with others without the mediation of images as symbols’. Just as Fechner once converted visual experience to numerical ratios (Crary 1990, 147), digital technology has dismantled it into matrices of pixels. And modern people outsource their memories to external devices such as smartphones (Henkel 2014, 401), never have a time to get a coffee and reflect on the more than ten thousand photographs buried in a sea of devices. This flood of ephemeral photographs will never bring us back to the film era, which once captured the world optically, nor does it signify the end of the game of symbolic reproduction played by 'mindless consumers of images’ (Stylianou-Lambert 2012, 1821) trained by Meta’s AI algorithms. Rather, as photography liquefies entirely into data and, within the fleeting cycle of being consumed and vanishing, continues to drive human memory and cognition ever further into the realm of ‘oblivion’, and when photography fully stops being a ‘tool of memory’ altogether, we are reduced to mere pure observers who ceaselessly consume and absorb the flow of vanishing images.
